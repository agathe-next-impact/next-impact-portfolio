## Special cases

### Consider headless despite a low score if:

- Direct competitors are adopting modern technologies (risk of falling behind)
- The target audience is highly digital (millennials, tech sector, B2B SaaS)
- Rapid growth in traffic or features is anticipated
- The goal is to make the technical platform sustainable for 5 years or more

### Stay on traditional despite a high score if:

- You want full autonomy and have no technical support
- The project depends on specific WordPress plugins with no headless equivalent (complex LMS, marketplace, etc.)
- The schedule does not allow for quality custom development
- The actual budget is not enough to cover both layers (backend + frontend)

### Option 1: Direct migration

- Full switch to the headless architecture
- Optimized total time, but a UAT phase is essential
- Recommended if the score is above 12

### Option 2: Progressive approach

Phase 1: optimize existing WordPress

- Caching, CDN, performance optimization
- Structure content with ACF to prepare API exposure
- Identify a vendor for frontend development

Phase 2: deploy a section in headless

- Migrate the home page or landing pages first
- The rest of the site stays on traditional WordPress
- Validate the architecture under real conditions

Phase 3: full migration

- Switch the entire site after validating phase 2
- Vendor is experienced and processes are mastered

### Option 3: Preparation without immediate migration

- Traditional WordPress optimized for the short term
- Identify a technical vendor and prepare the requirements document
- Build the budget and plan the migration in the medium term

## Questions to validate your decision

### Technical questions

1. "Are the limitations of my current WordPress structural (monolithic architecture) or circumstantial (poor configuration, unsuitable hosting)?"
2. "Can my vendor or team manage two production environments (WordPress + frontend)?"
3. "Do the measurable gains (performance, multi-channel, maintainability) justify the additional complexity?"

### Business questions

1. "Do my users or clients perceive the current limitations (slowness, dated design, missing features)?"
2. "Does the headless migration directly contribute to a measurable business goal (conversion rate, acquisition, retention)?"
3. "Is the return on investment estimable and realistic over 12-24 months?"

### Organizational questions

1. "Are the editorial and technical teams ready to adapt their workflow?"
2. "Does the schedule allow quality development (at least 8 weeks for a showcase site)?"
3. "Is long-term maintenance (updates, monitoring, evolution) ensured?"

## The final decision criterion

"Do the concrete, measurable benefits of headless (sub-second load times, custom design, multi-channel distribution) justify an investment 2 to 3 times higher and increased technical complexity?"

If yes: headless is the right architecture for your project.
If the answer is unclear: a well-optimized traditional WordPress (caching, CDN, performant hosting) will meet your needs.

## Conclusion

Headless and monolithic WordPress architectures are not opposed. They are two technical approaches suited to different contexts.

Traditional WordPress remains the optimal choice for the majority of websites: blogs, showcase sites, small e-commerce stores and projects with limited budgets.

Headless WordPress becomes relevant when requirements exceed what a monolithic architecture can deliver: sub-second performance, custom interfaces, multi-channel distribution.

Execution matters as much as the choice of architecture. A well-configured and optimized traditional WordPress will deliver more value than a poorly designed or under-resourced headless site.

If hesitation persists, start by optimizing your current WordPress to the maximum (caching, CDN, query optimization, suitable hosting). If those optimizations are not enough to meet your goals, headless will be the next logical step.
